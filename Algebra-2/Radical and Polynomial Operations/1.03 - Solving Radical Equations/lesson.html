1.03 — Solving Radical Equations

What reasoning and explanations can be used when solving radical equations, and how do extraneous solutions arise?

Understanding Radical Equations

A radical equation is an equation in which a variable is contained inside a radicand (under a radical sign) or is raised to a fractional exponent.

Solving these equations relies on a simple algebraic principle: inverse operations. To eliminate a root, we must raise both sides of the equation to the power equal to the index of the root.

The 3-Step Core Protocol

  1. Isolate: Manipulate the equation to get the radical term entirely by itself on one side of the equal sign.
  2. Eliminate: Raise both sides of the equation to the power of the radical's index. For square roots, square both sides; for cube roots, cube both sides.
  3. Solve & Verify: Solve the remaining equation using standard algebraic methods, and always check your solutions in the original equation.

Example 1: Basic Radical Equation

Solve the following equation:

$$ \sqrt{x + 7} - 2 = 0 $$

Step 1: Isolate the radical. Add $2$ to both sides:

$$ \sqrt{x + 7} = 2 $$

Step 2: Eliminate the radical. Square both sides:

$$ (\sqrt{x + 7})^2 = 2^2 \Rightarrow x + 7 = 4 $$

Step 3: Solve. Subtract $7$ from both sides:

$$ x = -3 $$

Step 4: Check. Plug $x = -3$ back into the original equation:

$$ \sqrt{-3 + 7} - 2 = \sqrt{4} - 2 = 2 - 2 = 0 \quad \text{(Valid!)} $$

The Danger of Extraneous Solutions

When solving radical equations, raising both sides to an even power (such as squaring) can introduce **extraneous solutions**. These are solutions that are mathematically derived during the process but do not actually satisfy the original equation.

Why do extraneous solutions happen?

Squaring a number loses its original sign. For example, both $3^2 = 9$ and $(-3)^2 = 9$. When we square both sides of an equation, we create a new "derived" equation that assumes both positive and negative values are equal, which can produce a false positive result.

Example 2: A Classic Extraneous Case

Solve the equation:

$$ x = \sqrt{x + 12} $$

Step 1: Eliminate the radical. The radical is already isolated, so we square both sides:

$$ x^2 = (\sqrt{x + 12})^2 \Rightarrow x^2 = x + 12 $$

Step 2: Set the quadratic to zero and factor.

$$ x^2 - x - 12 = 0 $$ $$ (x - 4)(x + 3) = 0 $$

This yields two potential solutions: $x = 4$ and $x = -3$.

Step 3: Test both solutions in the original equation.

  • Testing $x = 4$: $$ 4 = \sqrt{4 + 12} \Rightarrow 4 = \sqrt{16} \Rightarrow 4 = 4 \quad \text{(Valid Solution!)} $$
  • Testing $x = -3$: $$ -3 = \sqrt{-3 + 12} \Rightarrow -3 = \sqrt{9} \Rightarrow -3 = 3 \quad \text{(False! Extraneous Solution)} $$

Thus, the only real solution is $x = 4$. The value $x = -3$ is discarded.

Equations with Rational Exponents

Since rational exponents are just another way to write radicals, we solve them using the exact same logic. Our target is to raise both sides of the equation to the **reciprocal power** of the exponent to cancel it out.

Reciprocal Power Rule

$$ (x^{\frac{m}{n}})^{\frac{n}{m}} = x^1 = x $$

Crucial Rule for Even Numerators: If the numerator $m$ of the rational exponent is an even integer, raising both sides to the reciprocal power will require introducing a **plus-or-minus** sign ($\pm$) because of the even root rule.

Example 3: Solving with Fractional Exponents

Solve the following equation:

$$ 2x^{\frac{3}{2}} - 5 = 11 $$

Step 1: Isolate the exponential expression. Add $5$ and divide by $2$:

$$ 2x^{\frac{3}{2}} = 16 \Rightarrow x^{\frac{3}{2}} = 8 $$

Step 2: Raise both sides to the reciprocal power. The reciprocal of $\frac{3}{2}$ is $\frac{2}{3}$:

$$ (x^{\frac{3}{2}})^{\frac{2}{3}} = 8^{\frac{2}{3}} $$ $$ x = (\sqrt[3]{8})^2 $$ $$ x = (2)^2 \Rightarrow x = 4 $$

Step 3: Check. Plug $4$ into the original equation:

$$ 2(4)^{\frac{3}{2}} - 5 = 2(2)^3 - 5 = 2(8) - 5 = 16 - 5 = 11 \quad \text{(Valid!)} $$
